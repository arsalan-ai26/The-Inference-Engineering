Autoscaling and concurrency

Traffic to an AI product rises and falls through the day and spikes without warning. A fixed number of GPUs either wastes money in quiet hours or breaks the latency targets at peaks. Autoscaling adds and removes replicas to follow demand. For LLM inference it is harder than for web services: replicas are expensive, take minutes to start, and their capacity depends on prompt lengths and cache hits, not just request counts. This chapter shows how to measure what one replica can handle, which signals to scale on, how each autoscaling parameter trades cost against latency, and when scaling to zero makes sense.

1. Concurrency, not requests per second

Web services are often sized in requests per second. LLM replicas are better sized in concurrency: the number of requests being processed at the same moment, because that is what fills the batch and the KV cache. Little's law links the two:

requests in flight = arrival rate × average time in the system
Worked example

20 requests per second, each taking 6 s end to end (a 0.4 s TTFT and 200 tokens at 28 ms). In flight: 20 × 6 = 120. If one replica meets the SLO at up to 32 concurrent requests (from the load test in chapter 6.1), you need ⌈120 ÷ 32⌉ = 4 replicas, and more with headroom. If answers double in length, the same 20 requests per second need twice the concurrency and twice the replicas.

The concurrency target per replica comes from benchmarks, not from the engine's maximum. max-num-seqs (chapter 3.2) caps how many requests an engine will run; the target is how many it can run while meeting your SLOs, usually lower. The two must match: an autoscaler that thinks a replica handles 64 requests while the engine queues everything above 32 will scale too late.

Capacity is also bounded by KV memory: concurrency × average context × KV bytes per token must fit in the KV pool (chapter 2.4). For Llama 3.1 8B on an H100 with about 62 GB for KV at 128 KiB per token, contexts of 2,048 tokens allow about 230 sequences; contexts of 16K, about 29.

Practical policy: scale up on a traffic signal (requests or tokens in flight per replica against the concurrency target), and use queue length and latency as guard rails that trigger faster scale-up. Traffic signals lead; utilization lags. In Kubernetes this usually means the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler or KEDA reading the engine's Prometheus metrics, or a platform's own autoscaler.

3. The five parameters

ParameterMeaningRaising it
Minimum replicasAlways running, whatever the trafficFewer cold starts, more idle cost
Maximum replicasUpper bound (budget or quota)Survives bigger spikes, higher worst-case bill
Autoscaling windowHow long traffic is averaged before decidingSteadier decisions, slower reaction to spikes
Scale-down delayHow long demand must stay low before removing a replicaLess flapping, more spend after traffic falls
Concurrency target (with headroom)Requests per replica the autoscaler aims forFewer replicas, higher latency risk

Cold-start time (chapter 6.2) is the hidden sixth parameter: a replica requested now serves traffic only after it starts. Headroom (aiming for, say, 80% of the concurrency target) buys time for that.

4. Scale to zero, and when not to

Scale to zero removes every replica when there is no traffic and starts one when a request arrives. It needs fast cold starts and a queue that holds requests until a replica is ready. It suits development, internal tools used in business hours, and scheduled batch jobs. For a latency-sensitive product with light, unpredictable traffic, each "first request" waits a full cold start; the Kiely book's advice is that if you rely on scale to zero there, a pay-per-token API may be the better choice until traffic grows.

5. Scaling compound systems

Real products chain several models and CPU stages: speech-to-text, an LLM, text-to-speech; or retrieval, reranking and generation; plus CPU-heavy pre- and post-processing. Packaging them as one unit forces them to scale together, which leaves expensive GPUs idle while CPU stages run. Anyscale reports that production AI workloads often run well below 50% sustained GPU utilization for this reason. Scale each component on its own signal and hardware (a small GPU or MIG slice for a classifier, a full node for the LLM, CPUs for preprocessing), but keep them in the same cluster to avoid extra network hops.

Serving the long tail of models: token-level GPU pooling

A platform that hosts thousands of models (fine-tunes, older versions, niche languages) faces the opposite of the usual problem: most models get a request every few minutes. Alibaba Cloud Model Studio measured that more than 90% of its models are in this long tail, and that reserving GPUs for them put 17.7% of its GPUs on 1.35% of requests. Scale-to-zero does not fix this, because cold starts are far too slow for each request (section 4), and earlier multi-model systems fit only two or three models per GPU.

Aegaeon (Peking University and Alibaba, SOSP 2025) autoscales at the granularity of tokens: a GPU can switch which model it runs between scheduling steps, so many models share one pool. It splits the pool into prefill and decode partitions (chapter 4.6), and cuts the cost of each switch by 97% by reusing engine components, managing GPU memory explicitly and synchronizing KV caches at a fine grain. It supports up to seven models per GPU and 2 to 2.5 times higher request rates (or 1.5 to 9 times more goodput) than earlier systems. In a beta deployment of over three months serving tens of models from 1.8B to 72B parameters, the number of GPUs needed fell from 1,192 to 213, an 82% saving (authors-reported). The lesson for the five parameters of section 3: with enough models, the unit you autoscale is not a replica but a time slice of a shared GPU.

6. Expert layer

Predictive and scheduled scaling

When traffic follows a daily or weekly pattern, scaling ahead of time (from a forecast or a schedule) hides cold starts entirely. Reactive scaling then only handles deviations from the forecast.

Scaling disaggregated and multi-node replicas

With prefill/decode disaggregation (chapter 4.6), prefill and decode pools scale on different signals (queued prompt tokens and TTFT vs running sequences, KV usage and TPOT). Multi-node replicas (chapter 4.5) scale in large steps that must be provisioned as a group; leader/worker abstractions (for example LeaderWorkerSet in Kubernetes) treat them as one unit.

Load shedding

When demand exceeds even the maximum replicas, accepting every request makes everyone's latency terrible. Admission control (rejecting or deferring low-priority requests early, as Mooncake does with prediction-based early rejection) keeps the SLO for the requests you do accept (chapter 6.4).

7. Common mistakes

  • Scaling on GPU utilization alone.
  • A concurrency target taken from the engine's maximum instead of the measured SLO knee.
  • No headroom with multi-minute cold starts.
  • Scale-down delays so short that replicas flap during normal fluctuations.
  • Counting only ready replicas in cost, not starting ones.
  • Scaling a multi-stage pipeline as one container.
  • Scale to zero for a latency-sensitive product with sparse traffic.

8. Lab

Goal: size replicas from measurements and tune an autoscaling policy for bursty traffic.

Part A: size by hand

Traffic: 50 requests per second, 2,000 input and 300 output tokens. Your load test (chapter 6.1) says one replica meets p95 TTFT under 1 s and p95 TPOT under 40 ms up to 48 concurrent requests, with a typical request taking 0.5 s TTFT plus 300 × 30 ms. How many replicas at 80% headroom? How many if outputs grow to 900 tokens?

Answer

Time per request: 0.5 + 300 × 0.03 = 9.5 s. In flight: 50 × 9.5 = 475. Per replica at 80%: 48 × 0.8 = 38.4. Replicas: ⌈475 ÷ 38.4⌉ = 13. With 900 tokens: 0.5 + 27 = 27.5 s, 1,375 in flight, 36 replicas. Output length drives concurrency; also check that 48 sequences of up to 2,900 tokens fit in the KV pool.

Part B: an autoscaling simulator (CPU, seconds)

import math, random
random.seed(3)
def demand(minute):                     # requests in flight each minute: daily cycle + two bursts
    base = 60 + 50 * math.sin((minute / 1440 - 0.3) * 2 * math.pi)
    burst = 120 if 600 <= minute < 615 else 0
    burst += 80 if 1100 <= minute < 1130 else 0
    return max(0, base + burst + random.gauss(0, 6))

def simulate(target=16, headroom=0.8, window=5, down_delay=15, cold_start=6, min_r=2, max_r=20):
    ready, starting, hist, last_high = min_r, [], [], 0
    over, gpu_min = 0, 0
    for m in range(1440):
        d = demand(m); hist.append(d)
        ready += sum(1 for s in starting if s == m); starting = [s for s in starting if s > m]
        avg = sum(hist[-window:]) / len(hist[-window:])
        # TODO: want = replicas needed for avg at target * headroom, clipped to [min_r, max_r]
        have = ready + len(starting)
        if want >= ready: last_high = m
        if want > have:                                  # new replicas serve after the cold start
            starting += [m + cold_start] * (want - have)
        elif want < ready and m - last_high >= down_delay:
            ready -= 1
        if d > ready * target: over += 1
        gpu_min += ready + len(starting)                 # starting replicas cost money too
    return over, gpu_min / 60

for cfg in [dict(), dict(headroom=1.0), dict(window=1), dict(window=15),
            dict(cold_start=1), dict(cold_start=15), dict(down_delay=60)]:
    over, gh = simulate(**cfg)
    print(f"{str(cfg) or 'default':24s} minutes over capacity {over:4d}   replica-hours {gh:6.1f}")

No headroom saves 17% of the cost but runs over capacity for more than two hours. A 1-minute window reacts faster but chases noise (more replica-hours). Long cold starts double the over-capacity time at the same cost; short ones cut it. A long scale-down delay costs 9% more for no gain here. Now design a policy for the bursts: keep the default cost within 10% and get over-capacity minutes under 10.

9. In the real world

Autoscaling in production
  • Inference platforms scale on concurrency per replica with configurable windows, delays and minimum replicas, and invest heavily in cold starts because they set how lean a fleet can run.
  • Kubernetes stacks use KEDA or custom metrics adapters on engine metrics (waiting requests, KV usage), and gateways that queue during scale-up.
  • Large services mix reserved baseline capacity with on-demand and spot capacity for peaks (chapter 6.5).

11. Summary

  • Size replicas by concurrency (Little's law) using the measured SLO knee per replica, and check KV capacity for your context lengths.
  • Scale on leading traffic signals; use queue length and latency as guard rails; treat GPU utilization as a lagging hint.
  • Minimum and maximum replicas, window, scale-down delay and target with headroom trade cost against latency; cold-start time sets how much headroom you need.
  • Scale to zero only where first-request latency does not matter.
  • Scale compound pipelines per component, in one cluster.
