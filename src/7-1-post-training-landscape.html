The post-training landscape

A pretrained model has read trillions of tokens but does not yet behave like an assistant. Post-training turns it into one: supervised fine-tuning teaches the format and style of good answers, preference tuning teaches which of two answers is better, and reinforcement learning teaches behaviour that can be checked, such as correct maths or passing code. This chapter maps the stages, what each one changes inside the model, how much each costs, and the decision you will face most often as an engineer: prompt, retrieve, or fine-tune.

Answers
  1. Mostly behaviour: the chat format, when to stop, the tone and structure of answers, following instructions. Base and tuned models agree on most tokens; the differences concentrate in style and format tokens (section 3).
  2. Retrieve (RAG). The facts change monthly and must be citable; fine-tuning teaches behaviour much more reliably than it stores facts, and every update would need a new training run.
  3. It is cheap, stable and gives most of the gains. RL works best from a model that already follows the format and sometimes succeeds, because RL can only reinforce behaviour the model already produces.

1. The stages

Definitions

Pretraining: training on a very large text corpus to predict the next token. Produces a base model with knowledge and skills but no assistant behaviour.

Mid-training (continued pretraining): more next-token training on a targeted corpus, such as code, long documents or a company's documents, to add knowledge or context length.

SFT (supervised fine-tuning): training on example conversations so the model imitates the assistant's replies.

Preference optimization: training on pairs of answers labelled better and worse, so the model shifts probability toward the better kind (DPO and relatives, chapter 7.4).

RL (reinforcement learning): the model generates answers, a reward scores them, and training raises the probability of high-reward answers. RLHF uses a reward model trained on human preferences; RLVR uses verifiable rewards such as unit tests or checked maths answers.

Distillation: training a smaller student to imitate a larger teacher (chapter 7.5).

The usual order. Each stage starts from the previous checkpoint; most products need only SFT, and many need no training at all.

2. Why pipelines start with SFT

Hugging Face's account of post-training SmolLM3 gives three reasons almost every pipeline starts with SFT: it is cheap, it is stable ("it just works"), and a good SFT checkpoint gives most of the gains and makes DPO or RL far more effective afterwards. RL can only reinforce behaviour the model already sometimes produces; a base model that never follows the answer format gets no reward to learn from. The exception is the frontier, where no stronger teacher exists: DeepSeek trained R1-Zero with RL directly from a base model to discover reasoning behaviour that supervision could not provide. The DeepSeek-R1 paper (arXiv 2501.12948) reports that pure RL with verifiable rewards produced self-reflection, verification and strategy changes without human-written reasoning traces, and that these reasoning patterns could then be distilled into smaller models (chapter 7.5). For inference, it is also the paper that created today's long-output reasoning workload (chapter 8.1).

Two useful observations from the same work: preference optimization (they used APO, a DPO variant) raised instruction following on IFEval by 15 to 20 percentage points over their SFT checkpoint; and RL with verifiable maths rewards, applied naively, made the model's "no thinking" mode write long chains of thought to raise its reward, until a length penalty was added. Every stage optimizes exactly what you measure.

3. What post-training changes inside the model

Lin et al. compared base models with their aligned versions token by token: on most positions both models would pick the same token, and the shifts concentrate on stylistic tokens (discourse markers, formatting, the start of a reply, stop tokens). This supports the "superficial alignment" idea from LIMA, where 1,000 good examples gave strong alignment. Our lab repeats the measurement on Qwen2.5-0.5B and its instruct version: the base model ranks the instruct model's token first 78% of the time.

The practical reading: post-training is excellent at changing how a model answers and weaker at adding what it knows. New facts need mid-training on lots of text, or retrieval.

4. Prompt, retrieve or fine-tune?

A simple test from the Smol playbook: spend a few days building on a current strong open model. If prompting, examples and retrieval reach your target, you do not need to train. Fine-tuning makes sense when you have good domain data and a way to measure success.

5. What it costs, in inference terms

  • SFT and LoRA on a 7B to 8B model fit on one GPU for hours (chapter 7.3). The output can be served as merged weights or as a LoRA adapter on a shared base model (chapter 7.6).
  • Preference tuning needs a forward pass of a frozen reference model as well as the trained model, about twice the compute of SFT per example.
  • RL generates samples inside the training loop, so it is largely an inference problem: rollouts often dominate the time, and engines such as vLLM and SGLang serve them (chapter 8.1). Chapter 6.6 showed why the sampler and trainer must agree numerically.
  • On-policy distillation reached a higher maths score than RL at about one tenth of the GPU hours in the Qwen3 report (chapter 7.5).

6. Expert layer

Dense and sparse training signals

SFT gives a target for every token (dense) but only on someone else's text (off-policy). RL trains on the model's own samples (on-policy) but gives one number per answer (sparse), so it learns few bits per episode. On-policy distillation combines both: the student samples, and the teacher grades every token. That framing, from Thinking Machines, explains why each method costs what it does.

Hybrid reasoning models

Models with a thinking and a non-thinking mode need each mode's length distribution kept separate during RL. Hugging Face found joint RL on both modes unstable, one reason labs such as Qwen moved to releasing instruct and reasoning variants separately.

7. Common mistakes

  • Fine-tuning to add facts that change, instead of retrieving them.
  • Starting with RL before a working SFT baseline.
  • Training without an evaluation that measures the target behaviour (chapter 7.6).
  • Comparing a fine-tuned small model with an unprompted large one, instead of with the best prompt.
  • Forgetting that a tuned model must be served: adapters, versions, templates and quantization all apply (chapters 7.6 and 6.5).

8. Lab

Part A: what did post-training change?

Generate answers with Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, then ask the base model Qwen2.5-0.5B how it ranks each token the instruct model chose. Predict first: on what share of positions does the base model already rank the same token first?

import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")
inst = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct", dtype=torch.float32).eval()
base = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B", dtype=torch.float32).eval()
prompts = ["Give me three tips for writing clear emails.", "What is the boiling point of water at sea level?",
           "Explain what a hash table is.", "Write a haiku about the monsoon.", "How do I reverse a list in Python?"]
tot = top1 = top3 = 0; shifted = []
for p in prompts:
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": p}], add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt")
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = inst.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=80, do_sample=False)
        lb = base(out).logits[0, ids.shape[1]-1:-1]        # base model's prediction at each answer position
    for i, t in enumerate(out[0, ids.shape[1]:].tolist()):
        rank = (lb[i] > lb[i, t]).sum().item()              # 0 means the base model also ranks it first
        tot += 1; top1 += rank == 0; top3 += 0 < rank < 3
        if rank >= 3 and len(shifted) < 12: shifted.append(tok.decode([t]))
print(f"{tot} tokens: base top-1 {top1/tot:.0%}, top-2 or 3 {top3/tot:.0%}, shifted {1-(top1+top3)/tot:.0%}")
print(shifted)
What we measured (CPU)
answer tokens 337: base top-1 78%, base top-2/3 14%, shifted (rank>=4) 8%
examples of shifted tokens: [' conveying', 'Keep', ' but', ' email', ' This', ' and', ' under', ' on', ' At', ' gain', '<|im_end|>', ' allows']

Only 8% of positions shifted strongly, and they are mostly reply openers, connectives and the end-of-turn token: style and format, as Lin et al. found for larger models. (Depending on your Transformers version, apply_chat_template may return a dict; then use its input_ids.)

9. In the real world

Post-training in practice
  • Open model releases ship a base and an instruct (and often a reasoning) checkpoint; the instruct one went through SFT, preference tuning and usually RL.
  • Product teams mostly use prompting and RAG, and fine-tune small models for high-volume narrow tasks, structured extraction or latency.
  • Reasoning models rely on RL with verifiable rewards, which makes rollout generation a large inference workload.

11. Summary

  • Post-training stages: mid-training (knowledge), SFT (format and behaviour), preference tuning (quality judgements), RL (checkable skills), distillation (transfer to smaller models).
  • Start with SFT; add DPO or RL only with an evaluation that shows the need.
  • Post-training mainly changes style and behaviour; use retrieval or mid-training for knowledge.
  • Prompt and retrieve first; fine-tune when you have good data, a metric, and a cost, latency or control reason.
