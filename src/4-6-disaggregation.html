Prefill/decode disaggregation

Prefill is compute-bound and decides TTFT; decode is memory-bound and decides the time between tokens (chapter 2.5). On one GPU they compete: a long prompt arriving stalls every running decode. Disaggregation runs them on separate GPUs, moves each request's KV cache from the prefill GPU to a decode GPU, and lets each pool be sized and tuned for its own job. This chapter explains the interference, the KV transfer, how to size the two pools, and when the extra complexity pays off.

1. The problem: prefill and decode get in each other's way

With continuous batching (chapter 3.2), every engine step mixes decode tokens of running requests with prefill work for new ones. Chunked prefill caps how much prefill joins each step, but it cannot remove the conflict:

  • Decode slows when prefill arrives. A step that carries 2,048 prompt tokens does far more math than a pure decode step, so every running request waits longer for its next token. The DistServe authors report that one large prefill can inflate TPOT by 2 to 30 times under bursty load, even with chunked prefill.
  • Prefill waits behind decode. With a token budget, decodes go first. A full batch of decodes leaves little budget for new prompts, so TTFT rises.
  • One configuration must serve both. Prefill wants large token batches and modest parallelism; decode wants many sequences, lots of KV memory and often wider tensor parallelism for latency. A shared GPU must compromise, and the cluster must be sized for the worst case of both TTFT and TPOT at once.

2. How disaggregation works

  1. A router sends a new request to a prefill engine. It runs the prompt, produces the first token and the KV cache.
  2. The KV cache is transferred to a decode engine over NVLink, InfiniBand or RoCE, usually GPU memory to GPU memory with RDMA (no CPU copy).
  3. The decode engine adds the request to its running batch and generates the rest of the tokens.

Real deployments have several of each, written xPyD: 5P3D means five prefill engines and three decode engines for one model. Each pool scales on its own signal: prefill on queued prompt tokens and TTFT, decode on running sequences, KV memory and TPOT.

A common refinement is conditional disaggregation: the decode engine prefills locally when the prompt is short, or when most of it is already in its prefix cache (chapter 4.4), and sends only long, uncached prompts to the prefill pool. Short prompts never pay the transfer.

Definitions

Colocated (aggregated) serving: prefill and decode run on the same engine and GPUs.

Disaggregated serving: prefill and decode run on separate engines, usually separate GPUs or nodes.

Goodput: the request rate a system can serve while meeting its latency targets (for example p90 TTFT under 1 s and p90 TPOT under 50 ms). It is what disaggregation optimizes.

NIXL: NVIDIA's inference transfer library for moving KV blocks between engines and memory tiers; used by Dynamo and by vLLM's NixlConnector.

3. The KV transfer

The cost of disaggregation is moving the cache. Its size is the formula from chapter 2.4, and its time depends on the link:

transfer time ≈ prompt tokens × KV bytes per token ÷ link bandwidth
Interactive: KV transfer vs prefill time
Prefill time assumes the prefill engine runs at 40% of H100 BF16 peak on its GPUs (attention FLOPs included). If the transfer is much shorter than the prefill, it can be hidden by sending each layer's KV as soon as that layer is done.

Three ways to make the transfer cheap:

  • Layer-by-layer streaming. Send layer i's KV while layer i + 1 is still being computed, so most of the transfer overlaps with prefill.
  • Smaller caches. GQA, MLA and FP8 KV cut the bytes (chapter 4.2).
  • Placement. Keep prefill and decode engines on the same node or NVLink domain when possible; DistServe placed them according to cluster bandwidth for this reason.

Layouts can differ between pools: a prefill engine at TP 2 and a decode engine at TP 8 split the KV cache by heads differently. The transfer layer must re-shard (transpose) blocks on the way; Dynamo includes a kernel for this.

4. Sizing the pools: the xPyD ratio

Each pool must keep up with its share of the work, with headroom:

prefill GPUs ≈ arrival rate × prompt tokens ÷ (prefill tokens per second per GPU × target utilization)
decode GPUs ≈ arrival rate × output tokens ÷ (decode tokens per second per GPU at the TPOT target)

The ratio follows the workload. Long prompts and short answers (RAG, summarization, code context) need many prefill GPUs; short prompts and long answers (chat, reasoning) need many decode GPUs. Get it wrong and one pool queues while the other idles: a 1P1D setup with too little prefill capacity has stable TPOT but TTFT that grows without limit. Part C of the lab shows exactly this.

5. When disaggregation pays off

Disaggregation needs more GPUs, a fast network, a router, KV transfer, and two pools to monitor and scale. Practitioner guidance (from the Kiely book and from vLLM's own documentation) converges on:

  • High volume. Enough traffic to keep two pools busy: the Kiely book suggests starting at roughly 100 million to 1 billion tokens per day, depending on model size.
  • Large models. About 100B parameters and up, where each GPU is expensive and interference costs a lot.
  • Prefill-heavy traffic with strict decode targets. Long, varied, uncached prompts mixed with users who need smooth streaming. A coding assistant on a very large model, with many developers sending large code contexts, is the textbook case.

When these do not hold, more colocated replicas with chunked prefill and good prefix caching usually win. vLLM's documentation states it plainly: disaggregated prefill does not improve throughput; it lets you tune TTFT and inter-token latency separately and control tail latency.

6. Disaggregation in production stacks

Disaggregation went from a research idea (DistServe and Splitwise, both 2024) to a default building block within about eighteen months. DistServe reported serving up to 7.4 times more requests, or meeting 12.6 times tighter SLOs, than colocated systems of the time. Splitwise reported 1.4 times higher throughput at 20% lower cost, and studied running prefill and decode on different GPU types.

  • NVIDIA Dynamo (chapter 3.6): prefill queue, conditional disaggregation with thresholds on uncached prompt length and queue size, NIXL transfers with re-sharding, and runtime changes to the xPyD ratio.
  • vLLM (marked experimental): KV connectors such as NixlConnector, LMCacheConnector and MooncakeConnector, plus a proxy that sends the prefill request and then the decode request.
  • SGLang: PD disaggregation used for large DeepSeek deployments; the SGLang team reported DeepSeek-R1 on 96 H100s with 3 prefill nodes and 9 decode nodes.
  • llm-d and Ray Serve LLM: prefill and decode as separately scaled Kubernetes or Ray services.
  • DeepSeek described serving V3 with separate prefill and decode deployments, each using a different degree of expert parallelism. Its V4.1-Flash service (chapter 4.8) splits three ways, VE/prefill/decode (also called EPD): the vision encoder (VE) that turns images into tokens runs in its own pool on one cheaper GPU per instance, prefill on 8-GPU machines with small EP, decode on 64- to 128-GPU supernodes. Its open-source engine DeepInfer implements most of the engine side of this split, but not the KV cache system or the load balancers.
  • Huawei CloudMatrix-Infer (arXiv 2506.12708) scales prefill, decode and caching as three independent peer pools on a 384-NPU supernode, with KV cache access over the supernode's unified bus.

7. Expert layer

Different hardware per phase

Once the phases are separate, they can run on different GPUs: compute-rich chips for prefill, bandwidth- and memory-rich chips for decode. Splitwise explored H100 for one phase and A100 for the other; newer work and some hardware vendors design decode-specialized or attention-specialized accelerators along the same lines.

Attention/FFN disaggregation

A further split separates attention (memory-bound, holds the KV cache) from the feed-forward or MoE layers (compute-heavy, holds the weights) during decode, sending activations between them twice per layer. For large MoE models that already pay all-to-alls for expert parallelism, the extra communication is affordable. It is an active research and early production area: MegaScale-Infer (ByteDance) and Step-3 (StepFun) are the two published designs, and Huawei's xDeepServe supports MoE-attention disaggregation in production (chapter 4.5). Think of disaggregation as having two axes: along time (prefill vs decode, this chapter) and along the layer (attention vs experts); a large MoE service can use both.

Failure and backpressure

Disaggregation adds failure modes: a prefill finishes but the decode engine has no KV memory left, a transfer fails mid-way, or the prefill queue grows without bound. Production systems pull work rather than push it (decode engines accept transfers when they have room), bound the prefill queue, fall back to local prefill, and retry failed transfers (vLLM documents KV load failure recovery). Chapter 6.9 covers fault tolerance.

8. Common mistakes

  • Expecting disaggregation to raise raw throughput on a small model.
  • Using a fixed 1P1D ratio for prefill-heavy traffic, so prefill queues and TTFT explode.
  • Placing prefill and decode engines across a slow network without layer-wise streaming.
  • Disaggregating short or mostly cached prompts instead of prefilling them locally.
  • Watching only average latency; disaggregation's main benefit is in the tails (p90 to p99 TPOT).
  • Forgetting that decode engines can run out of KV memory under load; quantize and offload the cache (chapters 4.1 and 4.4).

import random
random.seed(0)
W, KVT, BW, FLOPS = 16.06e9, 131072, 3.35e12, 989e12 * 0.5
def step_time(n_decode, ctx_tokens, n_prefill_tokens):
    mem = (W + ctx_tokens * KVT) / BW
    comp = 2 * 8.03e9 * (n_decode + n_prefill_tokens) / FLOPS
    return max(mem, comp)

def decode_gpu(arrivals, prompt, out, budget=None):
    """arrivals: (time ready, original arrival). budget set = colocated with chunked prefill."""
    ttft, tpot, clock, queue, running, i = [], [], 0.0, [], [], 0
    while i < len(arrivals) or queue or running:
        while i < len(arrivals) and arrivals[i][0] <= clock:
            if budget: queue.append([arrivals[i][1], prompt])
            else:
                running.append({"arr": arrivals[i][1], "start": arrivals[i][0], "made": 1})
                ttft.append(arrivals[i][0] - arrivals[i][1])
            i += 1
        if not queue and not running: clock = arrivals[i][0]; continue
        chunk = 0
        if budget:                                   # decodes first, then prefill fills the budget
            left = budget - len(running)
            while queue and left > 0:
                take = min(left, queue[0][1]); queue[0][1] -= take; left -= take; chunk += take
                if queue[0][1] == 0: running.append({"arr": queue.pop(0)[0], "start": None, "made": 0})
        clock += step_time(len(running), sum(prompt + r["made"] for r in running), chunk)
        for r in running[:]:
            if r["start"] is None: r["start"] = clock; ttft.append(clock - r["arr"]); r["made"] = 1
            else: r["made"] += 1
            if r["made"] >= out: tpot.append((clock - r["start"]) / (out - 1)); running.remove(r)
    return ttft, tpot

Disaggregated decode is smooth: TPOT p99 barely moves from p50, because no prefill ever enters a decode step. Colocated TPOT more than doubles from 12 to 20 requests per second as more prefill chunks join each step. But at 20 requests per second, 2P2D has too little prefill capacity: each 4,000-token prompt takes about 130 ms, so two prefill GPUs handle at most about 15 per second, and the queue grows without limit. 3P1D keeps up, at the cost of more TPOT on its single decode GPU. The lesson: disaggregation trades TTFT for TPOT stability, and it only works with the right ratio. Try prompt = 500 and out = 1,000 (decode-heavy) and find the best split.

10. In the real world

Who disaggregates
  • Frontier and large open-model providers serving models with hundreds of billions of parameters, especially for coding and long-document products.
  • DeepSeek and Kimi (Mooncake) built their serving around separate prefill and decode clusters with KV-centric scheduling.
  • Inference platforms (Dynamo, llm-d, SGLang, Ray Serve LLM) offer it as a deployment pattern, usually with conditional disaggregation and autoscaling per pool.
  • Smaller deployments mostly stay colocated, with chunked prefill and prefix caching.

11. Check yourself

12. Summary

  • Colocated prefill and decode interfere: prefill inflates TPOT, decode delays prefill, and one configuration must serve both.
  • Disaggregation runs them on separate engines and moves the KV cache between them, usually by RDMA, often layer by layer.
  • Size the pools from prompt and output token rates; the xPyD ratio follows the workload, and a wrong ratio makes one pool queue.
  • It improves goodput and tail latency, not raw throughput; it pays off for large models, high volume and long uncached prompts.
  • Dynamo, vLLM, SGLang, llm-d and LMCache all support it; conditional disaggregation keeps short and cached prompts local.
