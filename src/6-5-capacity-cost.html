Capacity, deployment and cost

At scale, inference becomes a capacity business: getting enough of the right GPUs in the right places, keeping them running, changing models and engines without downtime, and knowing what each token costs. This chapter covers where GPUs come from and how they are bought, multi-region and multi-cloud serving, the reliability you should plan for, how to roll out a new model or engine safely, and how to build a cost model that compares dedicated deployments with per-token APIs honestly.

1. Where GPUs come from

HyperscalersAWS, Google Cloud, Azure, OracleIntegration with everything else, compliance, global regions; usually higher prices and quotas

Marketplaces and resellersVast.ai, SF Compute and othersCheapest, most variable reliability and terms
Own hardwareOn-premises or colocationLowest cost at high, steady utilization; capital, operations and lead times

Capacity is bought three ways:

  • Reserved: committed blocks for months or years at a discount. Cheapest per hour, but you pay whether you use them or not.
  • On-demand: by the hour, up to a quota. Flexible and expensive; scarce for the newest GPUs.
  • Spot (preemptible): discounted spare capacity that can be taken back at short notice. Good for batch jobs and extra replicas that can be lost.

Large deployments blend them: reserved capacity for the baseline load, on-demand for predictable peaks, spot for batch and burst. Live prices for common GPUs are in the compute guide; check them before each decision.

2. Multi-region and multi-cloud

A single cluster eventually runs out of GPUs, sits too far from some users, and fails as a unit. Serving from several regions and providers adds:

  • Capacity: pool GPUs wherever they are available.
  • Latency: run near users. Network round trips between continents are tens to hundreds of milliseconds, a real share of a TTFT budget.
  • Redundancy: survive a region or provider outage.
  • Compliance: keep data in its required country (data residency) and on certified providers.

The common architecture separates a control plane (deployments, global scaling, placement) from workload planes (one per cluster, serving traffic and scaling locally). Workload planes must keep serving if the control plane is down. A global load balancer sends each request to the nearest healthy region with spare capacity, and spills over to farther regions under load.

Two reliability postures: active-active (several regions serve at once; losing one shifts load to the others) and active-passive (a warm standby takes over on failure; cheaper but slower to switch and easy to let rot).

3. Plan for hardware failure

GPUs fail more often than most server hardware. The Llama 3 paper reports 419 unexpected interruptions during a 54-day training run on about 16,000 GPUs, mostly hardware (GPUs, HBM and related components), about one per 50,000 GPU-hours. A fleet of 1,000 inference GPUs at that rate sees roughly one failure every two days. Practices:

  • Health checks at the node level (driver errors, ECC errors, NVLink faults), not just the process level; cordon and drain unhealthy nodes.
  • Replicas spread across nodes and zones, so one failure removes one replica, not a service.
  • Retries for requests that fail mid-stream (with care: retrying a long generation doubles its cost), and multi-node replicas that restart as a group (chapter 6.9).

4. Changing models and engines safely

Model checkpoints, quantization recipes, engine versions and configurations change often, and each change can alter speed, cost or outputs. Test before shipping:

  • Offline: quality evaluations (chapter 4.1) and benchmarks on representative traffic (chapter 6.1).
  • Shadow traffic: copy a sample of live requests to the new version and compare latency, errors and outputs without serving its answers.
  • Load test at expected peak before cutover.
StrategyHow it worksExtra capacityRollback
Blue-greenA full new fleet; switch all traffic at onceA complete second fleetInstant (switch back)
Rolling updateReplace replicas a few at a timeSmall (one or a few replicas)Slow (roll forward or back again)
CanarySend a small share of traffic to the new version, watch metrics, raise the share in stepsSmall; the old fleet scales down as traffic movesFast (move traffic back)

For large GPU fleets, canaries are the usual choice: blue-green doubles GPU needs during the overlap. Two cautions: make sure the canary has enough replicas warm before it receives traffic (an autoscaled new deployment starts at its minimum), and watch the right metrics (TTFT, TPOT, errors, output length, quality signals) per version during the ramp. Combine with graceful draining (chapter 6.2) so no stream is cut.

5. Cost models

With a per-token API, cost is simple: tokens × price (with discounts for cached input and batch use). With dedicated GPUs, cost is GPU-hours × price, and the cost per token depends on throughput per GPU at your SLO and on how busy the GPUs are:

$ per million output tokens = GPU $/hour ÷ (output tokens/s per GPU at the SLO × 3,600 × average utilization) × 10⁶

Three levers move it by multiples: the GPU price, the throughput per GPU (hardware, quantization, batching, every optimization in Part 4), and utilization (traffic shape, autoscaling, headroom). Utilization is often the biggest and least visible: a fleet sized for peak traffic and idle at night may average 30%.

Worked example: size and price a service

50 requests per second, 2,000 input and 300 output tokens, p95 TTFT under 1 s, on H100s. A load test shows one H100 replica meets the SLO up to 48 concurrent requests, with about 0.5 s TTFT and 30 ms TPOT. Time per request: 0.5 + 300 × 0.03 = 9.5 s; in flight: 50 × 9.5 = 475; at 80% headroom 38.4 per replica: 13 replicas. Cost: 13 × 24 × $2.90 = $905 per day for 1.3 billion output tokens: about $0.70 per million output tokens, or about $0.09 per million total (input plus output) tokens. Add the night-time trough and the autoscaler's behaviour (chapter 6.3) to refine it.

Interactive: per-token API vs dedicated GPUs
Monthly cost of the same traffic on a per-token API and on dedicated GPUs. Throughput per GPU must come from your own load test at your SLO; the default is an idealized estimate for an 8B model in FP8 on an H100. Dedicated cost includes headroom and an engineering cost you set.

Run the widget at 0.5, 5 and 50 requests per second. Below some volume the API wins because you pay for idle GPUs and engineering; above it dedicated wins. Where the line falls depends on API prices, your throughput per GPU and your peak-to-average ratio. Compare total costs over at least a week of real traffic, not per-token prices reverse-engineered from GPU hours.

Total cost of ownership

Beyond GPU hours: engineering time to build and operate the stack, storage and network egress, observability, idle headroom, failed and retried requests, and the opportunity cost of slower model updates. Dedicated serving buys control (latency, privacy, custom models, predictable cost at scale); it is not automatically cheaper.

6. Expert layer

Bin packing across heterogeneous fleets

With many models and GPU types, capacity planning becomes bin packing: which model runs on which GPU type, at what parallelism, in which region, so that every SLO holds at minimum cost. Platforms solve this continuously as traffic and availability change; small models on MIG slices (chapter 5.5) and spare capacity used for batch jobs raise overall utilization.

Batch and offline traffic as a utilization tool

Requests that can wait hours (evaluations, embedding a corpus, synthetic data) fill the troughs of interactive traffic. Async and batch APIs with low priority (chapters 6.2 and 6.4) turn idle GPU-hours into useful work, raising average utilization and lowering the effective cost of the interactive service.

7. Common mistakes

  • Computing cost per token at 100% utilization.
  • Comparing an API's list price with a dedicated deployment's GPU cost and ignoring engineering, headroom and idle time.
  • Blue-green rollouts on large fleets without the capacity to run both.
  • Canary releases watched only for error rates, not latency, output length and quality.
  • Assuming one region is enough for global users.
  • No plan for GPU failure because "inference is stateless".

9. In the real world

Capacity and cost in practice
  • Inference platforms run multi-cloud control planes that place replicas wherever GPUs are available, with global routing and failover.
  • Large consumers reserve baseline capacity a year or more ahead and burst into on-demand and spot.
  • Teams moving from APIs to dedicated serving usually start with one high-volume use case where cost or latency justifies it, and keep APIs for the long tail.
  • Releases of new checkpoints and engine versions go through evals, shadow traffic and staged canaries.

11. Summary

  • Buy a blend of reserved, on-demand and spot capacity from hyperscalers, GPU clouds and markets; check live prices.
  • Serve from several regions and providers for capacity, latency, redundancy and compliance, with a control plane separate from workload planes.
  • Expect GPU failures and design health checks, spreading and retries for them.
  • Ship changes through evals, shadow traffic and canaries, with warm canary capacity and graceful draining.
  • Cost per token = GPU price ÷ (throughput at SLO × utilization); utilization and throughput matter as much as price; compare total costs with APIs over real traffic.

Notes on sources

  • The Kiely book and the day 25 README of 100 Days of Inference estimate about 5 ms per time zone and 15 ms one way from New York to San Francisco. Light in fiber alone needs about 20 ms one way over that distance, and real network round trips between the US coasts are typically 60 to 80 ms. Measure from your users' regions.
  • The day 26 README says INT4 is roughly 4 times cheaper per token than FP16 because each token reads a quarter of the weight bytes. That holds only where decode is memory-bound and dominated by weights; at large batches and long contexts the KV cache and compute limit the gain (chapter 4.1).
