Final capstone: defend a complete inference system

The last project brings the whole book together under one workload, one SLO and one budget. You design, build, measure and defend an inference system end to end: workload and quality target, model and hardware, a reproducible baseline, profiling, memory, scheduling and caching, precision and kernels, parallelism or disaggregation, routing, autoscaling and observability, failure testing and cost, and one engine-level change with ablations. Every claim carries an evidence label, negative results stay in, and someone else should be able to reproduce your numbers. This chapter gives the ten stages, the evidence rules, a defense template and the questions you should expect.

1. Evidence rules

Definitions

Evidence label: a tag on every number: measured (you ran it, with the config and raw data), simulated (from a model or simulator you built, with its assumptions), estimated (arithmetic from specifications), or quoted (from a source, with the reference).

Strong baseline: the best reasonable configuration of a standard system (for example a current engine with sensible flags), not a deliberately weak one.

Ablation: removing or changing one component to measure its contribution.

Negative result: a change that did not help, or helped in one regime and hurt in another; it is reported, not dropped.

The book has followed these rules: chapters mark CPU measurements as measured and GPU expectations as expectations, and notes call out sources that conflict. Hold your capstone to the same standard. A reviewer should be able to tell, for each number, whether to rerun it, check the arithmetic or look up the source.

3. A worked example of the first stages

Example: a support assistant for a bank
  • Workload: chat with a 1,500-token system prompt and retrieved context (shared across users), user turns of about 100 tokens, answers of about 250 tokens; 20 requests per second at peak, half that at night; open-loop, bursty arrivals.
  • SLO: p95 TTFT under 800 ms, p95 TPOT under 40 ms, 99.9% availability.
  • Quality: 500 held-out conversations scored for answer correctness and policy compliance; no more than 1 point of regression from the BF16 baseline (chapter 7.6's interval says 500 items detect about 3.5 points at 80%, so add a paired comparison).
  • Cost: under $0.50 per million output tokens at peak.
  • Choices to defend: an 8B instruct model (fine-tuned with LoRA on 3,000 conversations, merged in FP32), FP8 weights and KV cache on H100s, vLLM with prefix caching and session-aware routing, no disaggregation (prompts are mostly cached, so prefill interference is small; show it with the chapter 4.6 break-even model).
  • Engine change for stage 10: cache-aware admission that delays requests whose prefix is about to be cached by an in-flight request, measured at low, medium and high load.

Every number in such a plan starts as estimated or quoted, and must become measured or be labelled as not measured by the end.

4. The written defense

  1. Summary: workload, SLO, result (measured), cost (measured or estimated, labelled).
  2. System diagram: request path from client to GPU and back, with the components you chose.
  3. Decision log: for each choice, the alternatives considered, the evidence, and what result would change your mind.
  4. Results: baseline against final, Pareto curves, SLO compliance, quality, cost per million tokens.
  5. Engine change: what, why, the three ablations across three workload regions, and where it does not help.
  6. Failure tests: what you injected, what happened, recovery time.
  7. Limitations and negative results.
  8. Reproduction package: repository with configs, manifests, scripts, raw results, analysis notebooks and a README that reproduces the headline numbers.

Questions to expect

  • What was the bottleneck at each stage, and what evidence showed it?
  • Why this model size and precision, and what quality did you give up?
  • At what traffic level does your design stop meeting the SLO, and what scales next?
  • What happens to in-flight requests when a GPU fails, and how long until full capacity?
  • Which of your gains came from configuration and which from your code change?
  • What would you do with twice the budget, or half?
  • Which result surprised you, and what did you learn from it?

5. Independent reproduction

The strongest test of the package is someone else running it. Ask a colleague (or a fresh rented machine and an AI assistant following only your README) to reproduce one headline number. Record what they had to ask or fix; each question is a gap in the package. Report the reproduced number next to yours with its spread.

6. Common mistakes

  • A weak baseline that makes every change look good.
  • Numbers without labels, or simulated numbers presented as measured.
  • Optimizing before profiling.
  • Reporting only the regime where the change helps.
  • No quality evaluation after precision or kernel changes.
  • A reproduction package that only works on the author's machine.

7. The project

Complete the ten stages for a workload you care about (a client system, a product idea or the bank example). Budget GPU time by stage: most work (workload definition, simulation, analysis, writing) needs no GPU; rent GPUs for the baseline, the optimization runs, failure testing and the engine-change ablations, a few hours each, with scripts tested on CPU or a small GPU first (chapter 6.10's method). Deliver the written defense and the reproduction package, then present it in 20 minutes and answer the questions in section 4.
