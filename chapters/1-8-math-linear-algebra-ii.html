Math for papers II: rotations, low rank, orthogonalization and curvature

Chapter 1.1 covered vectors, dot products and matrix multiplication. Architecture and quantization papers lean on a second layer of linear algebra: rotations (RoPE, QuaRot), rank and low-rank factorization (LoRA, MLA), the singular value decomposition, orthogonalization (the Muon optimizer), doubly stochastic matrices (mHC) and second-order curvature (GPTQ, SqueezeLLM). This optional chapter explains each with one picture or one formula and checks it in NumPy.

Level: optional, for reading papers Time: 3 hours with the lab Needs: 1.1, 1.5 Compute: CPU, seconds
Before you start: skip check
  1. Why does RoPE make the attention score depend only on the distance between two tokens?
  2. A LoRA adapter of rank 16 on a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix has how many trainable parameters?
  3. What does Muon do to the singular values of an update matrix?
Answers
  1. It rotates queries and keys by an angle proportional to position. The dot product of two rotated vectors depends only on the difference of their angles.
  2. 16 × (4,096 + 4,096) = 131,072, about 0.78% of the matrix.
  3. It pushes all of them toward 1 (approximately replacing the update by its orthogonal factor UVᵀ), so no direction dominates.

2. Rotations and orthogonal matrices

Definitions

Orthogonal matrix: a square matrix Q with QᵀQ = I. Multiplying by it preserves lengths and dot products: (Qx)·(Qy) = x·y. Rotations and reflections are orthogonal.

2D rotation by angle θ: the matrix [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]]. Rotating by θ₁ then θ₂ is rotating by θ₁ + θ₂, and Rᵀ(θ) = R(−θ).

RoPE (chapter 2.3) splits a query or key of size d into d/2 pairs and rotates pair i by the angle position × ωi, with frequencies ωi = θ−2i/d. For a query at position m and a key at position n:

(R(mω) q) · (R(nω) k) = qᵀ R(mω)ᵀ R(nω) k = qᵀ R((n − m)ω) k

The score depends only on n − m: relative position from absolute rotations. Thinking of each pair as a complex number, rotation is multiplication by ei·pos·ω, which is how the RoFormer paper writes it. DeepSeek-V4 applies RoPE to only the last 64 dimensions, and also rotates its attention outputs by −i, because its compressed entries act as both keys and values (chapter 4.8). Lab Part A checks the relative property.

Interactive: RoPE turns positions into angles
One frequency pair. The query sits at position m and the key at position n; each is rotated by its position times ω. Shift both positions together and the angle between them, and so the score, stays the same.

QuaRot (chapter 4.1) uses the other property, that an orthogonal matrix changes nothing the model computes: multiply the hidden state by Q and the next weight matrix by Qᵀ, and the product is unchanged. With Q a normalized Hadamard matrix (entries ±1/√n, built recursively from [[1, 1], [1, −1]]), a vector with one large outlier becomes a vector whose energy is spread across all channels, so a single 4-bit scale fits it much better. Lab Part B: the outlier ratio drops from 11 to 1.5 and the INT4 error falls by about three times.

3. Rank, low-rank factorization and the SVD

Definitions

Rank: the number of independent directions a matrix can output; an m × n matrix of rank r can be written as the product of an m × r and an r × n matrix.

Singular value decomposition (SVD): any matrix W = U Σ Vᵀ, with U and V orthogonal and Σ diagonal with non-negative singular values in decreasing order. Each singular value says how much W stretches one direction.

Keeping only the largest r singular values gives the best rank-r approximation in the sense of least squared error (the Eckart-Young theorem). That fact sits behind three designs in the book:

  • LoRA (chapter 7.3) learns a weight change ΔW = BA with B of size d × r and A of size r × d. Its bet is that the useful fine-tuning update has low rank, so r of 8 to 64 is enough: r × 2d parameters instead of d².
  • MLA (chapter 2.4) stores a 512-value latent per token instead of full keys and values, and reconstructs them with up-projections: a learned low-rank factorization of the KV cache.
  • Query compression in DeepSeek-V4 (the ctQ latent, shared by the attention and the indexer queries) applies the same idea to queries.

Lab Part C builds a matrix that is nearly rank 32: rank-4 and rank-16 approximations leave most of it unexplained, rank 32 captures it almost fully, and going further buys little.

4. Orthogonalization: what Muon does

Every matrix has a polar decomposition G = (UVᵀ)(VΣVᵀ): an orthogonal-like part UVᵀ (all singular values set to 1) times a stretch. Muon (chapter 7.7) takes the momentum update G and applies (approximately) only UVᵀ, so every direction moves by a similar amount instead of a few large ones dominating. Computing an SVD each step would be slow, so Muon runs five steps of a Newton-Schulz iteration, a polynomial X ← aX + b(XXᵀ)X + c(XXᵀ)²X that pushes singular values toward 1 using only matrix multiplications, which GPUs do fast in BF16. Keller Jordan's coefficients (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315) deliberately leave the singular values between about 0.7 and 1.2 rather than exactly 1, which works as well in practice and converges faster. DeepSeek-V4 describes a "hybrid" Newton-Schulz variant in its Muon. Lab Part D runs the iteration.

5. Doubly stochastic matrices and Sinkhorn

A doubly stochastic matrix has non-negative entries and every row and column summing to 1; multiplying by it mixes values without growing or shrinking their total. Manifold-constrained hyper-connections (mHC) keep several parallel residual streams and mix them with such a matrix, so the residual signal neither explodes nor vanishes across layers. The Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm makes any positive matrix doubly stochastic by alternately dividing rows by their sums and columns by their sums; DeepSeek-V4 runs 20 iterations (tmax = 20). For inference this means a few extra small matmuls per layer, which is why DeepSeek fuses them (Mega-mHC, chapter 4.8). Lab Part E.

6. Curvature: the Hessian and second-order error

Definitions

Hessian: the matrix H of second derivatives of the loss with respect to the weights. It describes curvature: how fast the loss rises when weights move in each direction.

Second-order approximation: near a trained minimum the gradient is about zero, so changing the weights by e raises the loss by about ½ eᵀHe.

Quantization adds an error e to the weights. The formula says errors along high-curvature directions cost far more than the same errors along flat ones (lab Part F: the same 0.1 error costs 100 times more on the steep axis). GPTQ (chapter 4.1) uses this layer by layer: for a linear layer with inputs X, the Hessian of its output error is proportional to XXᵀ, and after rounding each weight it updates the remaining unrounded weights to cancel as much of ½ eᵀHe as possible. SqueezeLLM uses a diagonal approximation (the Fisher information, squared gradients) to decide which weights deserve more precision. You do not need to derive these algorithms to read the papers; you need to recognize that "Hessian-aware" means "weighted by how much the loss cares".

7. Expert layer

Kronecker products and preconditioners

Shampoo and SOAP (chapter 7.7) precondition a weight matrix's gradient with two small matrices, one for rows and one for columns, instead of one huge matrix over all entries. The combined preconditioner is their Kronecker product A ⊗ B, which never needs to be formed: (A ⊗ B) vec(G) = vec(B G Aᵀ). Muon can be read as a special case that keeps only the orthogonal direction.

Rank of attention and of the KV cache

Several KV compression papers start from the observation that keys across heads and positions have low effective rank (a few singular values carry most of the energy). The lab's SVD experiment is the measurement to run on a real model's keys before believing such a claim for your model.

8. Common mistakes

  • Applying RoPE with the wrong pairing (interleaved pairs vs first-half and second-half), which silently breaks attention; DeepSeek-V3.2's own demo code had this bug in its indexer, as its README noted in November 2025.
  • Assuming low rank: check the singular value spectrum first.
  • Reading "orthogonalized update" as "normalized update": normalizing scales all directions together, orthogonalizing equalizes them.
  • Forgetting that a rotation must be applied consistently to both sides (activations and weights), or the model's output changes.

9. Lab

Six short experiments in NumPy. Predict each before running.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Part A: RoPE is a rotation, so the q.k score depends only on the distance n - m
d, theta = 8, 10000.0
inv = theta ** (-np.arange(0, d, 2) / d)
def rope(x, pos):                                    # rotate each pair (x[2i], x[2i+1]) by pos * inv[i]
    a = pos * inv; c, s = np.cos(a), np.sin(a)
    x1, x2 = x[0::2], x[1::2]
    out = np.empty_like(x); out[0::2] = x1 * c - x2 * s; out[1::2] = x1 * s + x2 * c
    return out
q, k = rng.standard_normal(d), rng.standard_normal(d)
for m, n in [(3, 10), (103, 110), (5003, 5010)]:
    print(f"A  positions ({m},{n}): score {rope(q, m) @ rope(k, n):+.6f}   norm kept: {np.linalg.norm(rope(q, m)):.6f} vs {np.linalg.norm(q):.6f}")

# Part B: a Hadamard rotation spreads an outlier, so 4-bit quantization loses less
def hadamard(n):
    H = np.array([[1.0]])
    while H.shape[0] < n: H = np.block([[H, H], [H, -H]])
    return H / np.sqrt(n)
def int4_error(v):
    scale = np.abs(v).max() / 7; return np.linalg.norm(np.round(v / scale) * scale - v) / np.linalg.norm(v)
x = rng.standard_normal(128); x[5] = 60.0           # one outlier channel, as in real activations
Hm = hadamard(128)
print(f"B  max/RMS before {np.abs(x).max()/np.sqrt((x**2).mean()):.1f}, after {np.abs(Hm@x).max()/np.sqrt(((Hm@x)**2).mean()):.1f}")
print(f"   INT4 relative error before {int4_error(x):.3f}, after rotation {int4_error(Hm @ x):.3f};  H is orthogonal: {np.allclose(Hm @ Hm.T, np.eye(128))}")

# Part C: low rank. SVD gives the best rank-r approximation; LoRA trains a rank-r update
W = rng.standard_normal((256, 32)) @ rng.standard_normal((32, 256)) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal((256, 256))  # nearly rank 32
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(W)
for r in (4, 16, 32, 64):
    Wr = (U[:, :r] * S[:r]) @ Vt[:r]
    print(f"C  rank {r:3d}: relative error {np.linalg.norm(W - Wr)/np.linalg.norm(W):.3f}, parameters {r*(256+256):6,} vs {256*256:,} full")
d_model, r = 4096, 16
print(f"   LoRA on one 4096x4096 matrix at rank {r}: {r*2*d_model:,} trainable parameters = {r*2*d_model/d_model**2:.2%} of the matrix")

# Part D: Muon's orthogonalization. Newton-Schulz drives all singular values toward 1 (the U V^T factor)
G = rng.standard_normal((64, 32))
X = G / np.linalg.norm(G)                            # scale so singular values are at most 1
a, b, c = 3.4445, -4.7750, 2.0315                    # Keller Jordan's quintic coefficients
for step in range(1, 6):
    A = X.T @ X; X = a * X + X @ (b * A + c * A @ A)
    sv = np.linalg.svd(X, compute_uv=False)
    print(f"D  step {step}: singular values in [{sv.min():.3f}, {sv.max():.3f}]")
Ug, _, Vg = np.linalg.svd(G, full_matrices=False)
print(f"   distance to U V^T: {np.linalg.norm(X - Ug @ Vg)/np.linalg.norm(Ug @ Vg):.3f} (rough on purpose: Muon needs only singular values near 1)")

# Part E: Sinkhorn-Knopp makes a positive matrix doubly stochastic (rows and columns sum to 1), as mHC needs
M = np.exp(rng.standard_normal((4, 4)))
for it in range(20):
    M /= M.sum(1, keepdims=True); M /= M.sum(0, keepdims=True)
print("E  after 20 iterations, row sums", np.round(M.sum(1), 4), "column sums", np.round(M.sum(0), 4))

# Part F: second order. With error e in weights, the loss rises by about 1/2 e^T H e, so errors along high-curvature directions cost most
H2 = np.diag([100.0, 1.0])                           # curvature: steep along axis 0, flat along axis 1
for e in (np.array([0.1, 0.0]), np.array([0.0, 0.1])):
    print(f"F  weight error {e}: loss increase {0.5 * e @ H2 @ e:.4f}")

10. In the real world

Where this shows up
  • Engines ship fused RoPE kernels with model-specific pairing and scaling rules.
  • Quantization libraries offer Hadamard rotations (QuaRot) and Hessian-based GPTQ as standard options.
  • Training stacks now ship Muon next to AdamW; Kimi K2 and DeepSeek-V4 were trained with it.

12. Summary

  • Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and dot products: RoPE gets relative positions from rotations, QuaRot spreads outliers with Hadamard rotations.
  • The SVD gives the best low-rank approximation; LoRA, MLA and query compression are learned low-rank factorizations.
  • Muon replaces an update by approximately its orthogonal factor using a Newton-Schulz polynomial.
  • Sinkhorn normalization gives the doubly stochastic mixing matrices of mHC.
  • The Hessian weights quantization error: ½ eᵀHe; GPTQ and SqueezeLLM are built on it.
