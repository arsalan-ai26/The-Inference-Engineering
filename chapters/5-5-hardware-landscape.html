Part 5 · GPUs, hardware and kernels · Chapter 5.5

Hardware landscape

Which accelerator should serve your model? The answer depends on whether your workload is limited by memory capacity, memory bandwidth, compute, or interconnect, and on price, availability and software support. This chapter compares the NVIDIA generations you will meet (Ampere, Ada, Hopper, Blackwell, and the coming Rubin), explains MIG for small models, and surveys the alternatives: AMD Instinct, Google TPUs, AWS Trainium and Inferentia, and SRAM-heavy chips like Groq and Cerebras. It ends with a calculator that turns a model and a GPU into tokens per second and dollars per million tokens.

1. Reading a spec sheet for inference

Map each number to the bottleneck it controls (chapters 2.5 and 5.1):

  • Memory capacity decides what fits: weights plus KV cache (chapter 2.4). The Kiely book suggests leaving at least 50% headroom over the weights for KV in typical serving.
  • Memory bandwidth sets the decode speed at small and medium batch.
  • Dense tensor FLOP/s at your precision set prefill speed and large-batch decode. Use dense, not "with sparsity" figures, and compare at the same precision; FLOP/s roughly double with each halving of precision on hardware that supports it.
  • Supported formats (FP8 from Ada/Hopper, FP4 and MX formats from Blackwell) decide which quantization recipes (chapter 4.1) run at full speed.
  • Interconnect (NVLink generation, NVLink domain size, network cards) decides how well multi-GPU layouts scale (chapter 4.5).
  • Form factor: SXM modules with NVLink vs PCIe cards. PCIe versions of the same GPU often have lower power limits, less bandwidth and no NVLink.

What each generation added for inference:

  • Ampere: BF16 and TF32 tensor cores, MIG, NVLink 3. Still a cost-effective choice for models that fit and do not need FP8.
  • Ada (L4, L40S): FP8 tensor cores in a graphics-oriented, PCIe-only design. No NVLink, so poor for tensor parallelism; good for small and medium models, embeddings and vision.
  • Hopper: FP8, the Transformer Engine, TMA and warpgroup MMAs (chapter 5.2), NVLink 4. The H200 kept the compute and raised memory to 141 GB at 4.8 TB/s, which helps decode and long context directly.
  • Blackwell: FP4 and microscaling formats with hardware block scaling, tensor memory, about 2.4 times Hopper's bandwidth, NVLink 5 and rack-scale NVL72 systems.
  • Rubin (announced): HBM4 at much higher bandwidth, NVLink 6, the Vera CPU, and Rubin CPX, a separate part aimed at compute-bound prefill, matching the prefill/decode split of chapter 4.6.

New generations take roughly a year after release before kernels, engines and quantization recipes use them fully; reserve judgement until you can run your own benchmark.

Grace CPUs

GH200 and GB200 pair NVIDIA's Arm-based Grace CPU with the GPU over NVLink-C2C (up to 900 GB/s total), several times faster than PCIe. CPU memory becomes a practical tier for KV offload (chapter 4.4) and LoRA adapter storage.

3. Splitting a GPU: MIG

Small models (a few billion parameters) cannot use an H100's full compute and memory even at large batch. Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) splits an A100, H100, H200 or B200 into up to 7 isolated instances, each with its own SMs, memory, L2 slice and memory bandwidth share. On an H100 80 GB the main profiles are 1g.10gb (up to 7 per GPU), 2g.20gb (up to 3), 3g.40gb (up to 2), 4g.40gb and 7g.80gb. Seven compute slices exist because SM counts do not divide evenly; leftover SMs stay idle.

MIG gives hardware isolation (one tenant cannot slow another's memory), which time-sharing a GPU between processes does not. Use it for small models, embeddings and multi-tenant serving; do not use it for models that need the full memory or tensor parallelism.

Two patterns repeat across these designs. First, minimize data movement: reading DRAM costs far more energy and time than arithmetic, so many accelerators trade compute area for on-chip memory or replace caches with large software-managed scratchpads. Second, software is the moat: without CUDA's ecosystem every alternative must rebuild kernels, engines and quantization support, and its usefulness for you depends on whether your model and engine are supported on day one.

Huawei Ascend and the CloudMatrix384 supernode

Ascend is the main non-NVIDIA platform for frontier serving in China, and since September 2026 a first-class target of DeepSeek's open-source stack: FlashMLA, DeepEP, DeepGEMM, TileKernels and DeepSelect all ship Ascend 950 backends with the same Python APIs, and DeepInfer runs on Ascend 950 NPUs with CANN 9.2 (chapter 4.8). FlashMLA's README reports its sparse attention kernels at 410 TFLOP/s in prefill (95% of the hardware peak) and 360 TFLOP/s in decode (83%) on the Ascend 950.

The system matters as much as the chip. CloudMatrix384 (arXiv 2506.12708) puts 384 Ascend 910 NPUs and 192 Kunpeng CPUs on one Unified Bus network, so any NPU reaches any other directly and resources can be pooled: a scale-up domain several times larger than an NVL72 rack. Huawei's serving system on it ran DeepSeek-R1 with expert parallelism across 320 NPUs and reports 6,688 prefill and 1,943 decode tokens per second per NPU (decode under 50 ms TPOT); the production service paper (arXiv 2508.02520) reports 2,400 decode tokens per second per Ascend 910C at about 50 ms. Software is the price, as section 4 says: kernels must be ported (TileLang's second backend is one answer, chapter 5.4), and features can lag. DeepInfer's README lists batch-invariant inference and deterministic sampling as NVIDIA only.

5. Choosing hardware: a worked method

  1. Fit: weights at your precision plus the KV budget for your target concurrency and context (chapters 2.4 and 4.5).
  2. Phase mix: decode-heavy (chat, reasoning, agents) favours bandwidth; prefill-heavy (RAG, summarization, code context) favours FLOP/s; disaggregation lets you pick per phase (chapter 4.6).
  3. Precision: FP8 needs Ada/Hopper or newer; NVFP4 needs Blackwell.
  4. Scale-out: tensor parallelism needs NVLink; large MoE models benefit from large NVLink domains.
  5. Cost per token, not per hour: a GPU that costs twice as much per hour but serves three times the tokens is cheaper (chapter 6.5).
  6. Availability and support: can you get it in the regions you need, and does your engine support it well today?

6. Expert layer

Energy is the real constraint

At data-center scale, power and cooling limit how many accelerators can be installed. Moving a byte from DRAM costs orders of magnitude more energy than a multiply, and smaller number formats need far smaller multipliers (an FP8 multiplier needs a small fraction of the adders of an FP32 one). Both push hardware toward low precision and on-chip memory, and both make tokens per joule a metric that matters alongside tokens per dollar.

Why models look the way they do: DeepSeek's hardware papers

Two DeepSeek papers read hardware from the model builder's side. Fire-Flyer AI-HPC (SC'24, arXiv 2408.14158) describes a 10,000-GPU cluster built from PCIe A100s rather than NVLink systems, with a two-layer network, its own all-reduce (HFReduce) and file system (3FS); the authors report performance close to DGX-A100 at half the cost and 40% less energy. Insights into DeepSeek-V3 (ISCA 2025, arXiv 2505.09343) connects each V3 design choice to a hardware limit: MLA because HBM is scarce (70 KB of KV per token against 327 KB for Qwen-2.5 72B and 516 KB for Llama-3.1 405B), FP8 because tensor-core throughput doubles, node-limited routing because NVLink was about 4 times faster than InfiniBand on the H800, and the observation that wide-MoE decode speed is capped by interconnect bandwidth (about 67 tokens per second over 400 Gb/s InfiniBand in their idealized model, about 1,200 over an NVL72-class fabric). It ends with explicit requests to hardware vendors: more precise FP8 accumulation, native fine-grained scaling, merged scale-up and scale-out networks, lower-latency networking and memory-centric designs. Read it to predict what the next generation of models will assume about hardware (chapter 4.8).

Rack-scale systems change the unit of deployment

With GB200 and GB300 NVL72, the unit is a rack of 72 GPUs in one NVLink domain, with liquid cooling and its own failure modes. Wide expert parallelism and disaggregation are designed around this unit. Renting or operating one is a different exercise from renting 8-GPU nodes (chapter 6.7).

Local and edge hardware

Apple's M-series chips share one large memory between CPU and GPU (up to 512 GB on an M3 Ultra, at about 819 GB/s), so very large quantized models fit, at moderate speed. Consumer GPUs (an RTX 5090: 32 GB at about 1.8 TB/s) are faster but smaller. Mobile devices run small models with NPUs. Chapter 3.6 covered the runtimes.

7. Common mistakes

  • Comparing sparse FLOP/s of one GPU with dense FLOP/s of another.
  • Choosing by FLOP/s for a decode-heavy workload.
  • Renting PCIe H100s for tensor parallelism and expecting SXM performance.
  • Judging a new generation from launch-day numbers before kernels and engines catch up.
  • Comparing price per hour instead of price per token at your SLO.
  • Using a whole H100 for a 1B-parameter model instead of MIG slices or a smaller GPU.

Goal: estimate performance and cost across hardware from first principles, and compare with published numbers.

Part A: the 100 Days notebooks

Part B: decode speedup from bandwidth alone

For Llama 3.1 8B in BF16 at batch 1, estimate the decode floor (weights ÷ bandwidth) on an A100, H100, H200 and B200, and the speedup of each over the A100. Then do the same at batch 64 with 4K contexts (add the KV bytes). Why does the H200's advantage over the H100 grow at batch 64?

Answer

Batch 1 (16.1 GB): A100 8.0 ms, H100 4.8 ms, H200 3.3 ms, B200 2.0 ms; speedups over A100 1.0, 1.7, 2.4, 4.0. Batch 64 at 4K (KV 64 × 4,096 × 128 KiB = 34.4 GB, total 50.4 GB): A100 25 ms, H100 15 ms, H200 10.5 ms, B200 6.3 ms. The ratios stay the same in this idealized model, but the H200's larger memory lets it hold much larger batches and longer contexts than the H100, so at its best operating point it serves far more tokens per GPU.

9. In the real world

The current fleet
  • Most production LLM serving runs on Hopper (H100, H200) and increasingly Blackwell (B200, B300, GB200 NVL72).
  • Small models and embeddings run on L4, L40S, A10 or MIG slices.
  • AMD Instinct serves large models at several clouds and labs, with vLLM and SGLang support.
  • Specialized chips are mostly offered as APIs (Groq, Cerebras, SambaNova) rather than hardware you rent.

11. Summary

  • Read spec sheets by bottleneck: capacity (fit), bandwidth (decode), dense FLOP/s at your precision (prefill), formats, interconnect.
  • Hopper is today's workhorse; H200 and Blackwell add memory and bandwidth; Blackwell adds FP4; Rubin is announced with HBM4 and a prefill-oriented companion chip.
  • MIG splits large GPUs into isolated slices for small models.
  • AMD offers more memory per GPU; TPUs and Trainium are tied to their clouds; SRAM-heavy chips trade capacity for bandwidth; software support decides practicality.
  • Choose by cost per token at your SLO, after checking fit, phase mix, precision, interconnect and availability.
