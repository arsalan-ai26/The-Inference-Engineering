The serving ecosystem: multimodal, diffusion and staying current

Serving is no longer only text in, text out. Vision-language models turn images into hundreds of tokens, omni models chain encoders, language models and diffusion decoders in one request, and image and video generators run a large transformer dozens of times per output. Meanwhile engines ship new versions every few weeks and each major model arrives with its own serving recipe. This chapter covers what changes for each modality, and a method for keeping up.

Level: expert Time: 3 to 4 hours with the lab Needs: 2.5, 3.1 to 3.6 Compute: Colab T4
Before you start: what you should already know

This chapter assumes the prefill and decode cost model (chapter 2.5), prefix caching with multimodal hashes (chapter 3.5) and the engine landscape (chapter 3.6). Each section ends with what it means for capacity and latency.

1. Vision-language models: images become tokens

A vision-language model (VLM) adds a vision encoder in front of the language model. The encoder cuts the image into small patches, turns them into vectors, and a small projector maps those vectors into the language model's embedding space. To the language model they are just more tokens in the sequence.

The number of image tokens grows with resolution. Qwen2.5-VL, for example, gives roughly one token per 28 × 28 pixel tile after merging, so a 1,024 × 1,024 image becomes about 1,300 tokens, more than many text prompts. That changes serving costs in three ways:

  • Encoder compute runs once per image, before prefill, and adds to time to first token.
  • Prefill and KV memory grow with image tokens, exactly like long text (chapters 2.4 and 2.5).
  • Caching needs media hashes. Image placeholder tokens are identical for every image, so prefix caches include a hash of the image itself (chapter 3.5). Engines also cache encoder outputs: vLLM reports mm_cache_queries and mm_cache_hits.

Engines let you cap image resolution or the number of images per prompt, the main levers for VLM capacity. Some systems go further and run encoders on separate GPUs from the language model, the same idea as prefill-decode disaggregation (chapter 4.6), because encoders and language models have different compute and memory profiles.

2. Omni-modality pipelines

"Omni" models take and produce several modalities: text, images, audio and video. One request can pass through several different models: an audio or vision encoder, an autoregressive language model that reasons, and a diffusion or audio decoder that produces the output. Each stage has a different shape of work:

StageTypical shapeBottleneck
Encoders (vision, audio)One parallel pass per inputCompute
Autoregressive language modelPrefill, then token-by-token decodeCompute in prefill, memory bandwidth in decode
Diffusion decoders (images, video)Many full passes over a fixed-size latentCompute
Audio decoders (speech)Streaming chunks under tight latencyLatency per chunk

vLLM-Omni, released in late 2025, extends vLLM to these pipelines: it orchestrates heterogeneous stages within one request and applies vLLM's memory management to diffusion transformers as well as autoregressive models. Systems such as Cornserve take a similar view of multimodal serving as a graph of components that can be placed and scaled separately.

Capacity impact

Plan each stage separately. A pipeline is as fast as its slowest stage, and stages may want different GPUs and different batch sizes.

3. Diffusion model serving

Image and video generators work very differently from LLMs. A text-to-image pipeline such as FLUX.1-dev has four models: two text encoders (T5 and CLIP), a 12-billion-parameter transformer denoiser, and a VAE that decodes the final latent into pixels. Generation starts from random noise in a compressed latent space and runs the denoiser repeatedly, each step removing a little noise.

  • The denoiser dominates. It runs once per step (28 steps by default for FLUX.1-dev), over every latent token each time. One published walkthrough measured about 15.8 s per 1,024 × 1,024 image on an A100 and 6.9 s on an H100 before optimization, almost all in the denoiser.
  • It is compute-bound. Each step processes thousands of latent tokens at once (a 1,024 × 1,024 image is 128 × 128 in latent space, which FLUX packs into 2 × 2 patches: 4,096 image tokens plus the text tokens). Arithmetic intensity is high, like a large prefill (chapter 2.5).
  • There is no KV cache across steps in the LLM sense, and batching helps much less than for decode, because one image already fills the GPU.
Interactive: what an image costs
FLOPs per denoiser pass ≈ 2 × parameters × tokens, plus attention (chapter 2.2). Time ≈ steps × passes per step × FLOPs ÷ (GPU FLOP/s × efficiency). Defaults approximate FLUX.1-dev (12 B, 57 blocks, hidden size 3,072); change them to explore.

Ignores text encoders, VAE and communication. Parallel GPUs are assumed to split one image's work perfectly (an upper bound for sequence and guidance parallelism).

How serving systems make diffusion faster:

TechniqueWhat it doesCost
Fewer stepsBetter samplers and step-distilled models (1 to 8 steps)Quality depends on the model; needs a distilled variant
Caching between stepsReuse block outputs that change little from one step to the nextSmall quality changes; tuning per model
Guidance parallelismRun the two passes of classifier-free guidance on two GPUs at onceTwice the GPUs for up to 2 times lower latency
Sequence parallelismSplit the latent tokens across GPUs (Ulysses, ring attention), vital for video with tens of thousands of tokensCommunication per layer
Compilation, FP8, fused attentionThe same kernel-level levers as LLMs (chapters 3.1 and 4.1)Engineering and validation time

SGLang Diffusion (late 2025) brought SGLang's runtime to image and video models such as Wan, Hunyuan, Qwen-Image and Flux, with guidance parallelism and unified sequence parallelism, and reported 1.2 to 5.9 times speed-ups across workloads. vLLM-Omni offers diffusion acceleration in the vLLM ecosystem.

4. Model-specific serving recipes

New flagship models often need specific engine flags on day one. Engines publish recipes: tested commands and settings per model. Three examples from vLLM's recipes and blog show what they contain:

ModelWhat is specialWhat the recipe tells you
DeepSeek-V3.2DeepSeek Sparse Attention for long context; a much changed chat templateA dedicated tokenizer mode (--tokenizer-mode deepseek_v32), its own tool-call parser, 8-way parallelism, extra kernel packages
Xiaomi MiMo-V2-FlashMoE with 309 B total and 15 B active, hybrid attention, multi-token predictionSuggested --max-model-len (65,536 usually, 128K maximum) and --max-num-batched-tokens (32,768 for prompt-heavy work, lower for latency); notes that some features were not yet enabled
NVIDIA Nemotron 3 NanoHybrid Mamba-Transformer MoE with 1M-token context; later an NVFP4 version trained with quantization-aware distillationSupported precisions (BF16, FP8, NVFP4), and the reasoning and tool settings for agent use

The pattern: before serving a new model, read its recipe for the tokenizer and template mode, tool and reasoning parsers, parallelism, maximum lengths, quantized variants, and the list of features that do not work yet.

5. Around the engine: proxies and checks

  • Optimizing proxies. Projects such as optillm sit between the client and an OpenAI-compatible server and apply inference-time techniques (several samples and selection, iterative refinement and similar) without changing the engine. They trade more tokens per request for better answers on some tasks (chapter 2.7, test-time compute).
  • Guardrails in the request path. vLLM's semantic router project describes HaluGate, a token-level check that flags claims not supported by tool or retrieval results, using small classifiers rather than a second LLM as judge, so it adds little latency.
  • Local sparsity tricks. PowerInfer exploits the fact that a small set of "hot" neurons is active for most tokens: it keeps them on the GPU and computes the rarely active "cold" neurons on the CPU, to run large models on consumer hardware.

6. Staying current: a method

Engine releases arrive every few weeks, and defaults change (you have seen several in this Part). A sustainable method:

  1. Pin versions in production and in your notes (chapter 1.6). Never debug against "latest".
  2. Read release notes of the engines you run, for: changed defaults, new flags, deprecated flags, and new metrics names.
  3. Follow summaries. vLLM Daily publishes automated summaries of the day's merged changes; community newsletters (for example InferenceOps) cover vLLM, llm-d, KServe and the Kubernetes Gateway API Inference Extension together.
  4. Check hardware plugins if you run non-NVIDIA hardware: vLLM uses a plugin architecture for platforms such as Intel Gaudi, and each plugin has its own feature list and version lag.
  5. Re-benchmark after upgrades on your own workload (chapter 6.1). Performance can move in either direction.

The commercial side moves too. Companies now offer managed deployments built on the open engines, some founded by engine maintainers (for example Inferact around vLLM). Knowing who maintains what helps when you need support for a new model or a bug fix.

7. Expert layer

Unified autoregressive and diffusion models

Some models use one transformer for both text generation and image generation (for example Transfusion-style and Bagel-style designs), and some adapt autoregressive language models for parallel, diffusion-like decoding (block diffusion, chapter 2.7). Serving them needs schedulers that handle both kinds of step in one engine, which is part of why vLLM-Omni and SGLang Diffusion share code with their LLM engines.

Video is a long-context problem

A few seconds of video at moderate resolution becomes tens of thousands of latent tokens per denoiser pass. Attention cost (quadratic in tokens) dominates, so video serving leans on sequence parallelism, sparse or windowed attention and step caching, much as long-context LLM serving does.

Speech: latency per chunk

Real-time voice systems chain speech recognition, an LLM and speech synthesis, and users notice delays above a few hundred milliseconds. Each stage must stream: start producing output before the input is complete. That turns TTFT into the dominant metric for every stage.

8. Common mistakes

  • Sizing a VLM deployment from text prompt lengths and ignoring image tokens.
  • Expecting LLM-style batching gains from diffusion models, which are already compute-bound per image.
  • Serving a new model with generic flags instead of its recipe, then reporting bad quality or broken tool calls.
  • Upgrading an engine and assuming last month's tuning still holds.
  • Treating every stage of a multimodal pipeline as one unit for scaling.

9. Lab

Goal: measure what images cost a VLM, and what steps cost a diffusion model. Predict before each run.

Part A: image tokens in a VLM (T4)

# Terminal or background process:
# vllm serve Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct --dtype half --max-model-len 8192 --limit-mm-per-prompt '{"image": 1}'
import base64, io, time, requests
from PIL import Image
def ask(size):
    img = Image.new("RGB", (size, size), (120, 160, 200))
    buf = io.BytesIO(); img.save(buf, format="PNG")
    url = "data:image/png;base64," + base64.b64encode(buf.getvalue()).decode()
    body = {"model": "Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct", "max_tokens": 1,
            "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": [{"type": "image_url", "image_url": {"url": url}},
                                                      {"type": "text", "text": "Describe the image."}]}]}
    t = time.perf_counter(); r = requests.post("http://localhost:8000/v1/chat/completions", json=body).json()
    return r["usage"]["prompt_tokens"], time.perf_counter() - t
for s in [224, 448, 896, 1344]:
    n, dt = ask(s)
    print(f"{s}x{s}: {n} prompt tokens, {dt*1000:.0f} ms to first token (1 output token)")

Predict first: if one token covers about 28 × 28 pixels, how many tokens should a 896 × 896 image add? How should TTFT grow with resolution? Compare your numbers with the prediction. (If the model's processor resizes images to a limit, the largest sizes will stop growing; find that limit.)

Part B: diffusion steps (T4)

import torch, time
from diffusers import AutoPipelineForText2Image
pipe = AutoPipelineForText2Image.from_pretrained("stabilityai/sd-turbo", torch_dtype=torch.float16, variant="fp16").to("cuda")
prompt = "a small robot reading a book in a library, watercolor"
pipe(prompt, num_inference_steps=1, guidance_scale=0.0)          # warm-up
for steps in [1, 2, 4]:
    torch.cuda.synchronize(); t = time.perf_counter()
    image = pipe(prompt, num_inference_steps=steps, guidance_scale=0.0).images[0]
    torch.cuda.synchronize(); print(f"{steps} steps: {time.perf_counter() - t:.2f} s")
    image.save(f"robot_{steps}.png")

SD-Turbo is a step-distilled model designed for 1 to 4 steps, without classifier-free guidance (hence guidance_scale=0.0). Predict: does time grow linearly with steps? What part of the time does not depend on steps? Look at the images: does quality improve with more steps for this model?

Part C: read a recipe

Pick a model released in the last three months. Find its serving recipe in the vLLM or SGLang documentation. List: the flags it needs beyond the defaults, the parsers it uses, the recommended maximum length, the parallelism it assumes, and any features marked as not yet supported. Estimate its memory footprint with chapters 1.3 and 2.4 and check it against the recipe's GPU count.

10. In the real world

How the ecosystem is used
  • Document and screenshot understanding runs on VLMs, and image tokens are often the largest part of the prompt.
  • Image and video products serve diffusion models with step-distilled variants, caching and multi-GPU parallelism to reach acceptable latency.
  • Model launches ship with engine recipes on day one; platform teams adopt them and then tune.
  • Platform teams track engine releases and community newsletters as part of normal operations.

11. Check yourself

12. Summary

  • VLMs turn images into many tokens through a vision encoder; resolution sets prefill cost and KV memory; caches need media hashes.
  • Omni pipelines chain encoders, language models and diffusion or audio decoders; plan and scale each stage.
  • Diffusion serving is dominated by repeated, compute-bound denoiser passes; reduce steps, cache between steps, and parallelize guidance and sequence.
  • New models need their recipes: tokenizer modes, parsers, limits and parallelism.
  • Proxies and guardrails extend serving without changing the engine.
  • Pin versions, read release notes and summaries, and re-benchmark after upgrades.
