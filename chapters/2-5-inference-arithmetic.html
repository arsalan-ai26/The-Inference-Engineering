Inference arithmetic

With five numbers (model size, bytes per weight, context, batch, and two GPU specs) you can predict how fast a model will run, before you rent a GPU or write any code. This chapter teaches that estimate: FLOPs, bytes, arithmetic intensity, the roofline, and the two efficiency scores (MFU and MBU) that tell you how close a real system comes to the limit.

Level: core, with an expert layer Time: 4 to 5 hours with the lab Needs: 1.1, 1.3, 2.2, 2.4 Compute: Colab T4

Answers
  1. About 989 TFLOP/s ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 295 FLOPs per byte. An operation must do at least that much math per byte it reads to keep the math units busy.
  2. Around a batch of 295 tokens per step, because each BF16 weight (2 bytes) gives 2 FLOPs per token: about 1 FLOP per byte per token. Reading the KV cache pushes the real crossover even higher.
  3. 14 GB read in 14 ms is 1 TB/s, so MBU ≈ 50%.

1. Two limits on every GPU

A GPU has two resources that matter for inference:

  • Compute: how many floating-point operations it can do per second (FLOP/s).
  • Memory bandwidth: how many bytes per second it can move between its main memory (HBM) and its compute units.

Every operation needs both: it reads data, then computes on it. Whichever takes longer sets the time. If reading takes longer, the operation is memory-bound: the math units wait. If computing takes longer, it is compute-bound: the memory system waits.

Definitions

Ops:byte ratio (of hardware): peak FLOP/s ÷ peak bytes/s. How many FLOPs the GPU can do in the time it reads one byte.

Arithmetic intensity (of an operation): its FLOPs ÷ the bytes it must read and write.

The rule: if intensity < ops:byte, the operation is memory-bound. If intensity > ops:byte, it is compute-bound.

The ops:byte numbers are in the hundreds. That means the math units are enormously faster than memory. Most of inference engineering is about feeding them.

2. The roofline

Plot achievable speed (FLOP/s) against arithmetic intensity. The limit has two parts:

achievable FLOP/s = min( peak FLOP/s , bandwidth × intensity )

On a log-log chart, the bandwidth part is a rising diagonal and the peak is a flat roof. The corner where they meet is at intensity = ops:byte. Operations to the left of the corner are memory-bound and can only go faster with more bandwidth or fewer bytes. Operations to the right are compute-bound and can only go faster with more FLOP/s or fewer FLOPs.

3. Decode, counted

In one decode step, each sequence in the batch adds one token. The two big costs:

Weight matmuls

Every weight is read once per step and used once per sequence. With P parameters, b bytes per weight and a batch of B sequences:

FLOPs = 2 × P × B bytes ≈ P × b intensity ≈ 2B ÷ b

In BF16 (b = 2), the intensity is about B FLOPs per byte. At batch 1 it is about 1. An H100 needs about 295. So at small batch, decode uses well under 1% of the GPU's math, and its speed is set only by how fast the weights can be read. The batch size where the weight matmuls reach the corner is the critical batch size:

B_crit ≈ ops:byte × b ÷ 2 (≈ 295 for BF16 weights on an H100)

Attention over the cache

Each sequence also reads its own KV cache (chapter 2.4). For one query head, attention over a context of n tokens does about 4 × n × d FLOPs and reads the 4 × n × d bytes of BF16 keys and values of its KV head. So its intensity is about 1 for MHA, and about the group size g for grouped-query attention, because g query heads share one read. Crucially, this does not grow with batch: every sequence reads its own cache. Attention in decode stays memory-bound at any batch size.

Worked example: Llama 3.1 8B on one H100, BF16
  • Batch 1, short context. Bytes per step ≈ 16.06 GB. Time ≥ 16.06 ÷ 3,350 GB/s = 4.8 ms. Ceiling ≈ 209 tokens/s. FLOPs per step ≈ 16 GFLOP, which takes the math units only 0.016 ms: they are idle 99.7% of the time.
  • Batch 64, 2K context. KV per sequence = 131,072 × 2,048 = 0.27 GB; × 64 = 17.2 GB. Bytes per step ≈ 33.2 GB → 9.9 ms. FLOPs ≈ 64 × 16 GFLOP = 1.03 TFLOP → 1.0 ms at peak. Still memory-bound. Ceiling ≈ 6,450 tokens/s in total, about 101 per user.
  • Lesson: going from batch 1 to 64 multiplied total throughput by about 31 while each user slowed by only about 2 times. This is why every server batches.

4. Prefill, counted

Prefill runs T prompt tokens through the model in one pass. The weights are read once and used T times:

FLOPs ≈ 2 × P × T (+ attention, chapter 2.2) intensity ≈ 2T ÷ b

For prompts longer than a few hundred tokens, prefill is compute-bound. Its time is FLOPs ÷ (peak × efficiency). For a 2,048-token prompt into Llama 3.1 8B: 2 × 8.03 × 10⁹ × 2,048 ≈ 32.9 TFLOP. At 50% of an H100's 989 TFLOP/s, about 66 ms. Long prompts make time to first token (TTFT) grow linearly from the matmuls and, beyond tens of thousands of tokens, quadratically from attention.

Watch out: "attention is the most expensive operation"

You will read that attention is the most expensive part of both prefill and decode. At short and medium context it is not: the weight matmuls dominate the FLOPs and the bytes. Attention takes over in prefill only beyond tens of thousands of tokens (chapter 2.2 widget), and in decode once the total KV cache read exceeds the weight read (chapter 2.4, lab Part D).

Watch out: decode attention intensity

A well-known worked example computes attention with Q, K and V all of shape [4096, 128], and gets about 62 FLOPs per byte, then calls it decode. With a full [4096, 128] query block that is prefill attention, done without a fused kernel. In decode the query is one row, and the intensity is about 1 (MHA) or g (GQA), whatever the context length. Another popular tutorial gives "about 31 FLOPs per byte at 4,096 tokens" for decode attention; that is also too high for MHA.

5. MFU and MBU: how close to the limit?

Real systems do not reach peak. Two scores measure how close they get:

MFU (model FLOPs utilization) = achieved FLOP/s ÷ peak FLOP/s
MBU (model bandwidth utilization) = achieved bytes/s ÷ peak bytes/s

Achieved FLOP/s counts the model's useful FLOPs (2 × P per token, plus attention), not extra work the system did. Achieved bytes/s counts the weights plus the KV cache that one step must read, divided by the step time.

  • Use MBU for memory-bound work: decode at small and medium batch.
  • Use MFU for compute-bound work: prefill, and decode at very large batch.
Worked examples

MBU. A 7B model in BF16 (14 GB) has a TPOT of 14 ms at batch 1. Achieved bandwidth = 14 GB ÷ 0.014 s = 1 TB/s. On a 2 TB/s GPU, MBU = 50%.

MFU. The same model processes a 4,000-token prompt in 400 ms. FLOPs ≈ 2 × 7 × 10⁹ × 4,000 = 56 TFLOP. Achieved = 140 TFLOP/s. On a 312 TFLOP/s GPU, MFU ≈ 45%.

Good production engines reach roughly 50% to 80% MBU in decode and 40% to 70% MFU in prefill. Very low values point to overheads: Python, kernel launches, small inefficient kernels or communication.

6. A five-minute estimate, step by step

  1. Weight bytes = parameters × bytes per weight (chapter 1.3).
  2. KV bytes per token = 2 × layers × KV heads × head size × bytes (chapter 2.4).
  3. Decode step memory time = (weight bytes + batch × context × KV bytes per token) ÷ (bandwidth × MBU).
  4. Decode step compute time = 2 × parameters × batch ÷ (peak FLOP/s × MFU).
  5. TPOT ≈ the larger of the two, plus a fixed overhead per step.
  6. TTFT ≈ queue time + 2 × parameters × prompt tokens ÷ (peak × MFU).
  7. Throughput = batch ÷ TPOT tokens per second.

This estimate is usually within a factor of 2 of a well-tuned engine. When your measurement is much worse, the gap tells you where to look.

7. Expert layer

The third regime: overhead-bound

For small models or tiny batches, neither compute nor memory is the limit. The GPU waits for the CPU to launch the next kernel. Each launch costs microseconds, and a model step can launch hundreds of kernels. Symptoms: MBU far below 50% and GPU-Util that looks busy. CUDA graphs and compilation (chapter 5.7) remove most of this. Lab Part C shows it on a T4.

Lower precision moves the corner

Quantized weights cut b. With weight-only INT4 and BF16 math, decode bytes fall 4 times while FLOPs stay the same, so a memory-bound decode can run up to 4 times faster, and B_crit falls to about a quarter. With FP8 weights and FP8 math, both bytes and peak FLOP/s change by 2, so B_crit stays about the same. Chapter 4.1 uses this to decide which quantization fits which workload.

Multi-GPU arithmetic

Splitting a model over N GPUs with tensor parallelism divides the weight and KV bytes per GPU by N, so memory-bound decode can approach N times faster. Each layer then needs communication between GPUs, which adds time that grows with batch and shrinks with faster links such as NVLink. Chapter 4.5 adds that term to the estimate.

Speculative decoding raises intensity

Checking k guessed tokens in one pass turns one-token decode into a small k-token prefill: the weights are read once for k tokens, so intensity rises about k times. When decode is memory-bound, this can give several accepted tokens for close to the cost of one step (chapter 4.3).

Energy follows bytes

Moving a byte from HBM costs far more energy than doing one multiply-add on it. Memory-bound decode therefore spends most of its energy on data movement. This is part of why performance per watt improves so much with batching and lower precision.

8. Common mistakes

  • Using sparse FLOP/s figures from marketing as the dense peak.
  • Forgetting the KV cache bytes in decode estimates at large batch or long context.
  • Calling decode compute-bound at batch 64 on an H100. For BF16 weights the crossover is near 300, and the KV cache pushes it higher.
  • Computing MFU from total GPU work including padding or recomputation. Count the model's useful FLOPs only.
  • Comparing MBU of systems at different batch sizes without saying so; MBU naturally falls as batch grows.

Part C: measure decode on a T4 and compute MBU

import torch, time
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer
name = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name); tok.padding_side = "left"
m = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name, torch_dtype=torch.float16).cuda().eval()
W = sum(p.numel() * p.element_size() for p in m.parameters())          # weight bytes
kv_per_tok = 2 * m.config.num_hidden_layers * m.config.num_key_value_heads * (m.config.hidden_size // m.config.num_attention_heads) * 2
BW, PEAK = 320e9, 65e12                                                  # T4 data sheet

@torch.inference_mode()
def decode_rate(B, new=128):
    ids = tok(["Tell me a long story about a robot."] * B, return_tensors="pt", padding=True).to("cuda")
    m.generate(**ids, max_new_tokens=8, do_sample=False)                 # warm-up
    torch.cuda.synchronize(); t = time.perf_counter()
    out = m.generate(**ids, max_new_tokens=new, min_new_tokens=new, do_sample=False)
    torch.cuda.synchronize(); dt = time.perf_counter() - t
    step = dt / new
    ctx = ids.input_ids.shape[1] + new / 2                                # average context
    mbu = (W + B * ctx * kv_per_tok) / step / BW
    mfu = 2 * (W / 2) * B / step / PEAK
    return B * new / dt, step * 1e3, mbu, mfu

for B in [1, 4, 16, 64]:
    tps, ms, mbu, mfu = decode_rate(B)
    print(f"B={B:3d}  {tps:8.0f} tok/s  step {ms:6.2f} ms  MBU {mbu:5.1%}  MFU {mfu:5.2%}")

Predict first: the ceiling from Part B is about 3 ms per step. How close will Hugging Face generate get at batch 1?

What to expect and why

The measured step will likely be many times slower than 3 ms, so MBU will be low (often below 20%). The model is small, so each step is dominated by Python and kernel-launch overhead: the overhead-bound regime from the expert layer. As the batch grows, step time barely changes, so tokens per second rise almost linearly: the same shape as the widget, but with a fixed overhead floor. Part D of chapter 3.1 repeats this with vLLM, which removes most of the overhead.

Part D: a measured roofline

def matmul_point(m_, k=4096, n=4096):
    A = torch.randn(m_, k, device="cuda", dtype=torch.float16); B = torch.randn(k, n, device="cuda", dtype=torch.float16)
    for _ in range(3): A @ B
    torch.cuda.synchronize(); t = time.perf_counter()
    for _ in range(20): A @ B
    torch.cuda.synchronize(); dt = (time.perf_counter() - t) / 20
    flops = 2 * m_ * k * n; byts = 2 * (m_ * k + k * n + m_ * n)
    return flops / byts, flops / dt
pts = [matmul_point(m_) for m_ in [1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024, 4096]]
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
x = [p[0] for p in pts]; y = [p[1] / 1e12 for p in pts]
xs = [0.5, 2000]
plt.loglog(xs, [min(PEAK, BW * v) / 1e12 for v in xs], "k--", label="T4 roofline")
plt.loglog(x, y, "o-", label="measured matmul"); plt.xlabel("FLOPs per byte"); plt.ylabel("TFLOP/s"); plt.legend(); plt.show()

Your points should follow the diagonal at low intensity and bend under the roof at high intensity. The gap to the roof is the kernel's inefficiency at that shape.

10. In the real world

How teams use this arithmetic
  • Hardware choice: for chat-style decode, memory bandwidth per dollar matters more than FLOP/s per dollar. For long-prompt, short-answer work (summarization, classification), FLOP/s matters more.
  • Benchmark sanity checks: a vendor claim of more tokens per second at batch 1 than bandwidth ÷ weight bytes allows is a red flag (unless it uses speculative decoding or quantization).
  • Engine tuning: MBU and MFU tell an engineer whether to work on kernels and overhead (low efficiency) or on batching and quantization (high efficiency but slow anyway).
  • Pricing: providers set prices per million tokens from the throughput they can reach at their batch sizes, which this arithmetic estimates.

12. Summary

  • Ops:byte = peak FLOP/s ÷ bandwidth (≈ 295 for H100 BF16). Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs ÷ bytes of an operation. Below the ratio: memory-bound.
  • Roofline: achievable = min(peak, bandwidth × intensity).
  • Decode weight matmuls have intensity ≈ 2B/b; B_crit ≈ ops:byte × b/2. Decode attention has intensity ≈ g and never improves with batch.
  • Prefill has intensity ≈ 2T/b and is compute-bound for prompts longer than a few hundred tokens.
  • TPOT ≈ max(compute time, memory time) + overhead; TTFT ≈ 2 × P × T ÷ (peak × MFU).
  • MBU scores memory-bound work; MFU scores compute-bound work. Low values mean overhead.
